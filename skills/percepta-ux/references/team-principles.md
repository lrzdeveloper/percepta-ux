# Team principles: making a value decide something

## Build a usable rule

A product principle expresses a team's priority in a real tradeoff. A psychological
observation helps explain behavior. A local rule specifies an application. These
are different levels: the psychology does not scientifically prove a team's
chosen item limit, wording length, or layout.

For each proposed principle, capture:

- The recurring product decision and the users it affects.
- The priority and the alternative it gives up in that context.
- The relevant psychological mechanism and evidence from this product, if any.
- A concrete application rule, an example, and a justified exception.
- A way to check the outcome, an owner, and a reason to revisit the rule.

“Be intuitive” or “look modern” cannot settle a contested choice. A useful
principle should produce a different decision in at least one realistic scenario
without becoming a one-screen workaround. Make it memorable enough to use in
reviews, and narrow enough that it has a meaningful boundary.

## Develop and adopt

Invite contributors whose work and perspectives are affected, including adjacent
disciplines and user/access perspectives where feasible. Align on the decisions
and success criteria. Generate candidate priorities independently, share them,
cluster themes, and converge through discussion or voting. Votes reveal team
preference, not user validation. Refine the candidates by applying them to actual
unresolved design choices and inspecting conflicts.

Circulate the resulting rules in the team's working system and put relevant
examples close to components and reviews. Visibility and short show-and-tell
sessions can build shared vocabulary; posters alone do not demonstrate adoption.
Capture revisions from real decisions rather than expanding a slogan collection.
The objective is consistent delegated judgment, not a new gatekeeper.

## Original example

**Context:** warehouse operators scan items repeatedly while handling interruptions.

**Priority:** keep the next inventory action recognizable during interruptions.

**Mechanisms:** Jakob supports learned placement; Miller supports retaining
task context; Doherty supports prompt, understandable feedback.

**Local application:** after a scan, retain the item, quantity, and save status;
keep the next scan action in the established location. An exceptional review
state explains the blocker and preserves the scanned item.

**Exception:** a consequential stock correction needs deliberate review before
commitment. The familiar action location does not override error prevention.

**Check:** observe resumed tasks after interruptions, wrong-item corrections,
and lost context. Adjust the rule if it creates repeated mistakes.

This is a team rule to test in its own product. It is not a universal obligation
for every scanner or a numerical consequence derived from a psychological law.

Source basis: ch. 11, printed pp. 131–138; visibility, show-and-tell, collaborative
