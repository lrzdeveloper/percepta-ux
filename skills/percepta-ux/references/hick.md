# Hick: making a choice understandable

## Mechanism and boundary

Choosing among alternatives costs processing time. Number, similarity, clarity,
and the person's knowledge influence the decision. Hick–Hyman originates in
reaction-time tasks; its equation does not automatically model every search,
purchase, or expert workflow. Choice overload and decision satisfaction are
related questions, not identical outcomes or guaranteed effects.

The book expresses response time as `RT = a + b log2(n)`, where n counts stimuli
and a and b are task-dependent measured constants. State the experimental
assumptions before applying a quantitative model. Counting menu entries without
measuring interpretation or search does not produce a validated time estimate.

Useful reduction improves the path to the person's goal. It can mean grouping,
prioritizing, comparing, or sequencing rather than deleting options. Less visible
content can increase effort if people must guess what an icon means or where
something was hidden.

## Diagnose and act

- Identify the actual decision and the information needed to make it. Separate
  alternatives from content that explains them. State consequential differences
  in comparable terms.
- Group choices using audience terminology; test ambiguous group membership.
  Choose card sorting for classification questions, then test findability with
  realistic tasks. A team's preferred categories are not user evidence.
- Reveal relevant choices at the appropriate time. Keep the next meaningful
  step clear, retain backtracking and state, and account for extra navigation.
  Shortcuts may remain visible for frequent expert tasks.
- Add labels or contextual explanation where symbols are ambiguous. Save visual
  space through hierarchy before removing the words people need to understand.
- A recommendation should have a real user-centered basis and leave alternatives
  usable. Show enough about price, commitment, and consequences to support an
  informed choice. Preselection is not proof of preference.

When simplification hides essential work or consequences, use [Tesler](tesler.md).
When emphasis or defaults steer consent or spending, read [Ethics](ethics.md).
Avoid universal claims such as “three plans always convert best.”

## Map the current decision

For an unclear screen, identify the goal, the decision needed now, competing
choices, information needed to compare them, the next action, and the way back.
Distinguish different user goals from several controls competing for the same goal.
A dashboard with independent tasks need not become a linear wizard.

When one task dominates, give its next action clear visual priority. Keep secondary
actions findable, label them meaningfully, and retain refusal, correction, and exit.
One dominant action is a hierarchy choice, not a law requiring one option on every
screen. Do not bury search, expert shortcuts, price differences, or cancellation
just to lower a visible count.

For optional advanced controls, progressive disclosure can reduce initial
interpretation. Show a recognizable entry, preserve entered state, and count the
extra reveal/navigation work. Compare simultaneous and staged versions on the
same realistic task before claiming the shorter-looking version is easier.

## Verify

Measure relevant-choice success, decision time, wrong turns, and comprehension.
Include novices and experts when both matter. For a purchase, faster decisions
with more unintended commitments are a poor outcome. Pair speed or conversion
with understanding and reversal/refund signals.

## Original example

A document export offers twelve controls with overlapping labels. First ask
whether people are choosing a destination, a format, or a quality level. Present
those decisions coherently and reveal technical options when needed, while
retaining a direct path for repeat exports. Collapsing everything into an unlabeled
gear would reduce visible choices and increase interpretation work.

Source basis: ch. 4, printed pp. 39–50; decision complexity, paradox of choice,
card sorting, progressive learning, and oversimplification. Evaluation boundaries
and the operational example here are Percepta extensions.
