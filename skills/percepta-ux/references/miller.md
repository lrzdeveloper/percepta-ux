# Miller: remembering versus recognizing

## Mechanism and boundary

Working memory is limited and sensitive to familiarity, distraction, and task
demands. Meaningful chunks can make information easier to process. Chunking
groups units a person understands as related; drawing a border around arbitrary
items does not create a useful semantic unit.

The famous 7 ± 2 is not a menu limit, a fixed measure of working-memory capacity,
or a requirement to show seven things. Visible options can be recognized without
being recalled. Hiding them may replace scanning with search and remembering.
Later research also questions a universal count of chunks. Assess this audience
and task rather than choosing a more fashionable numeric cap.

## Diagnose and act

- Find what must be held in mind: a code between screens, a selected item while
  comparing options, constraints during a calculation, or information lost when
  going Back. Externalize relevant state and preserve entered work.
- Group content by the user's concepts: items with their attributes, steps with
  their outcomes, or related commands. Use headings, alignment, proximity,
  spacing, and consistent order to make relationships clear.
- Make dense material scannable with a useful hierarchy and readable lines.
  Retain enough context to interpret each group. Meaningful density may help
  experts more than a sparse layout that adds navigation.
- Use formatting that aids comprehension while allowing paste and accessible
  reading. A formatted identifier should not become several troublesome inputs
  merely to resemble chunks.

Separate memory burden from choice complexity: a long visible categorized menu
can have little recall burden but poor findability. Use [Hick](hick.md) when the
decision or classification is the obstacle, and [Tesler](tesler.md) when the system
exports work that it could handle.

## Verify

Ask participants to complete a real task without an explanation of the grouping.
Look for lost context, backtracking, repeated rereading, transcription errors,
and the need to remember information that could remain visible. Compare task
success and effort before imposing new limits on item counts.

## Original example

A purchasing flow shows delivery constraints on one screen and asks the user
to choose a supplier on the next. Keep the applicable constraints beside the
supplier choices and preserve comparison state. Reducing ten suppliers to five
would not solve the memory dependency.
