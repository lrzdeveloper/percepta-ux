# Ethics: support an intended outcome

## Frame the decision

Name the user's intended outcome and the business objective separately. Ask
whether the intervention helps the person reach that outcome with understanding
and control. Increased engagement, conversion, or retention does not by itself
establish benefit. Consider people affected indirectly and those outside the
idealized happy path.

Psychology can explain susceptibility; it does not supply consent or justify
exploitation. Protective friction can reduce mistakes, protect privacy, and
support deliberation. Remove unnecessary effort while keeping useful agency.

## Review the behavior-shaping mechanism

| Mechanism | Inspect | Prefer |
|---|---|---|
| Intermittent variable rewards | Repeated checking without a useful endpoint; reward uncertainty used mainly to prolong consumption | Predictable value, useful status, notification control, and a natural stopping point |
| Infinite loops/autoplay | Automatic continuation without a renewed decision | Clear continuation controls, stoppability, and completion cues |
| Social affirmation | Pressure, visible comparison, anxiety, or unnecessary notification | User-controlled visibility and notification preferences; relevant feedback |
| Personalization | Data dependence, opacity, narrowed exposure, incorrect or sensitive inferences | Explainable relevance where useful, editable preferences, and a less personalized path |
| Defaults | Commitments or disclosures accepted without awareness | A visible, understandable initial choice suited to user expectations; explicit review for meaningful commitment |
| Removal of friction | Accidental purchases, sharing, deletion, or loss of control | Proportionate review, undo, or confirmation based on consequence |
| Reciprocity | A gift, contact suggestion, or favor used to create social obligation | Clear optionality without punitive or shame-based language |
| Deceptive patterns | Hidden costs, disguised ads, false urgency, forced permission, obstructed refusal or exit | Honest terms, comparable comprehension, usable refusal, and a dependable exit |

These patterns require contextual assessment. A feed, personalization, or a
default is not automatically harmful; document the actual mechanism and who
bears the cost. Avoid simplistic neuroscience claims or treating broad social
correlations as proof of harm caused by this particular product.

## Inspect the consequences

Test declining, backing out, canceling, removing permission, exporting work,
deleting data, losing access, and recovering from a mistake where relevant.
Compare effort and clarity between entering and exiting a commitment. Give
legitimate necessary checks a specific reason; avoid adding persuasion steps
that obstruct the decision the person has already made.

Consider vulnerable contexts, unexpected use, harassment/abuse possibilities,
shared devices, accessibility needs, interrupted work, and downstream exposure.
Use diverse participants and team perspectives to find blind spots. Scale this
review to the feature's actual consequences rather than imposing a formal
checklist on every cosmetic change.

Pair a business metric with user success and harm guardrails: informed task
completion, comprehension, unwanted commitments, early reversals, complaints,
unresolved failures, or unwanted notifications as relevant. Ask users about
effects that telemetry cannot explain. Record owner and follow-up criteria for
meaningful risks. Data collection itself should be proportionate and authorized.

## Original example

A subscription flow improves initial conversion by obscuring recurring cost and
making cancellation difficult. Keep the recurring price, renewal timing, and
decision visible; make refusal and cancellation understandable and operable.
Then compare informed enrollment, early cancellations, disputes, and satisfaction.
The legitimate business objective remains; accidental commitment is not counted
as proof of successful UX.

## Protective friction

Use specific consequence text, preview, scoped confirmation, or reversible undo
when helpful. Do not simulate security work, invent progress, or add arbitrary
waiting to appear trustworthy. A result can stay visible long enough to understand
without claiming that the system is still working. Distinguish necessary
verification from exit obstruction.
