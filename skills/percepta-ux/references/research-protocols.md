## Personas and mental models

Use research-grounded segments to represent task, motivation, knowledge,
constraints, expectations, and pain points. Record the underlying observations
and uncertainty. Speculative personas can organize hypotheses but cannot validate
them. Include diverse contexts and affected people; a persona's age or decorative
biography is useful only if it changes the design decision.

Elicit expectations before demonstrating behavior: “What would you expect to
happen here?” Compare the person's account with observed task behavior. Distinguish
what they believe the system does from what it actually does.

## Contextual inquiry

Use when real work, environment, interruptions, physical reach, collaborators,
or external tools may explain the problem better than recall alone.

Agree on scope and observation conditions. Watch a real task in its natural
setting, let the participant control interruption timing, and ask open questions
about relevant actions. Record tools, workarounds, handoffs, and nonstandard
cases. Separate observation from your interpretation; check the interpretation
at a suitable moment and summarize it at the end for correction. Observation
can change behavior, and a small sample cannot establish prevalence.

## Interviews

Use for goals, terminology, expectations, context, and remembered experiences.
Choose a focused research question and a flexible discussion guide. Build rapport
without making assumptions about a person's life. Ask for recent concrete
examples: “Tell me about the last time you corrected an invoice.” Follow up on
sequence, obstacles, tools, and consequences. Avoid questions that supply the
desired answer or ask people to predict hypothetical future use.

Self-report explains perspective; it does not establish actual task performance.
Combine interviews with observation when memory or stated preference is inadequate.

## Card sorting

Use for audience classification, category names, and grouping expectations.
Prepare representative topic cards without accidentally implying the categories
through repeated wording. In an open sort, participants group and name topics;
in a closed sort, they place topics into supplied groups. Hybrid formats can test
partly established structures. Ask about ambiguous or unsorted cards and the
reasoning behind groups.

Analyze patterns and disagreement by audience. A card sort informs taxonomy;
it does not prove navigation findability. A subsequent task-based tree test or
navigation study is a Percepta extension for checking the resulting structure.

## Chunking

This is an information-design technique, not a participant-recruitment method.
Group by task-relevant meaning, make relationships legible, retain context, and
check scanning and comprehension with realistic material. Use [Miller](miller.md)
to separate recognition from recall. Judge the grouping by understanding rather
than by whether it contains a prescribed number of items.

## Journey mapping

Use for end-to-end expectations, emotional peaks, handoffs, and recovery. Define
the lens: audience/persona, scenario, and expected outcome. Map phases, actions,
questions, constraints, tools, touchpoints, and evidence of emotional state.
Keep actual and anticipated journeys separate. Add opportunities with ownership
and outcome measures. A visually smooth emotional curve drawn by the team is
not user data. Validate uncertain points and include failure and exit paths.

## Usability testing

Use to learn whether representative people can perform a real task with the
current design. Define study goals, representative recruitment, realistic tasks,
prototype limitations, and success/failure criteria. Prompts should describe an
outcome without revealing the target control's label or path.

Remain neutral, observe before intervening, and record assistance. Think-aloud
can reveal reasoning but can also alter timing; interpret those measures with
that limitation. Observe task success, mistakes, recovery, and effort alongside
self-reported ease or appeal. Test the design, not the participant. Record device,
input method, and assistive technology when relevant.

Re-test meaningful revisions. A small formative sample can reveal concrete
problems but does not establish a precise population rate or commercial uplift.
For comparative claims, consider order effects, comparable fidelity, task
differences, and adequate sampling. Do not invent a universal sample-size rule.

## Eye tracking

Use only when a gaze-related question, appropriate equipment, calibration, and
representative tasks justify it. Choose measures that answer that question:
time to fixation in a defined area, fixation sequence/duration, or comparative
gaze allocation. Heatmaps aggregate attention patterns and can hide individual
paths. Gaze plots preserve sequence but still require context.

Calibration quality, sample, stimuli, task framing, and cultural context affect
interpretation. Looking does not establish comprehension, preference, emotion,
intent, or consent. Combine gaze evidence with behavior and interviews. If the
tools are unavailable, offer an appropriate task-based observation plan; never
label a guessed heatmap as an actual eye-tracking result.

## Progressive disclosure

An interaction technique for revealing detail at the point of need. Select a useful
baseline, keep a discoverable labeled route to the rest, preserve state when
expanding or returning, and support input methods beyond hover. Test whether
people can discover the extra controls and whether frequent expert tasks slow
down. Essential consequences belong before the decision, not behind optional
detail. Use [Tesler](tesler.md) for complexity allocation.

## Synthesis and decisions

Collect all relevant observations before deciding the pattern. Separate a quote,
an observed action, an interpretation, and a proposed solution. Group evidence
into themes, examine contradictory cases, and involve varied perspectives when
available. State method, audience, limitations, insight, supporting evidence,
implication, and next decision. An unresolved question can be a valid finding.

Pair quantitative outcomes with qualitative explanations. For a proposed change,
choose a measure of user success and a guardrail for unintended harm. An increase
in clicks or session length alone does not establish improved experience.
